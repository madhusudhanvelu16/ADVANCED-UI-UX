Abstract

Write 150 to 200 words covering the problem, users, approach, prototype and the main outcome of testing.

Solo late-night commuting creates acute vulnerability for ride-hailing passengers. Conventional safety features—loud sirens and bright flashing banners—fail in confined vehicle cabins because they escalate driver aggression and collapse under perpetrator coercion.

This project presents SafeRide, an advanced mobile-first night safety interface designed across five engineering stages: Discover, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test. SafeRide establishes three core defense pillars: (1) Zero-Audio Covert Triggers featuring a 1.5-second radial hold, secret multi-tap gestures, and pocket volume rocker simulation; (2) Dual-PIN Coercion Defense separating accidental deactivation (Normal PIN: 1234) from hostage coercion (Duress PIN: 9999), which displays a deceptive green trip screen while covertly streaming priority distress telemetry; and (3) an Autonomous AI Route Shield with a 30-second countdown for detour detection.

Built with React 18, Tailwind CSS, and Leaflet, SafeRide underwent rigorous usability testing with 5 real users (including Shreyas M, Sivanesan, Yuvan, Samarth, and Divya). Testing confirmed a 100% completion rate, sub-3-second activation (median 2.4s), zero accidental audio spills, and an outstanding 89.5/100 System Usability Scale score.

1. Problem Definition and Scope

Stage: Discover. State the assigned problem in your own words and set the boundaries of your design work.

Problem statement

Solo night commuters who feel intimidated or endangered in a ride-hailing cab lack a discreet, panic-free method to summon assistance without alerting an aggressive driver. Current in-app emergency buttons sound audible alarms or flash bright red screens that provoke driver hostility. Furthermore, if a driver coerces the passenger to unlock their phone and dismiss the alarm, conventional single-PIN platforms cancel emergency response immediately, leaving the victim completely unprotected.

Stakeholders and target users

The primary users are solo late-night ride-hailing commuters, university students, and shift workers traveling between 9:00 PM and 6:00 AM. Secondary stakeholders include emergency guardians and family members who require passive real-time trip tracking. Tertiary stakeholders include platform safety dispatchers and emergency response desks (National Helpline 112).

Goals and success metrics

The primary design goals are: (1) Sub-3-second covert distress activation from pocket or screen; (2) Zero audible chimes or involuntary screen luminance; (3) 100% coercion defense where entering a Duress PIN deceives the attacker while escalating silent emergency response; (4) Greater than 80% reduction in accidental false alarms via a 1.5-second radial hold threshold; (5) System Usability Scale (SUS) score exceeding 85/100 and WCAG 2.2 AA contrast compliance.

Constraints and assumptions

The interface must operate as a mobile web application (React/Vite/Tailwind) without requiring native mobile OS rooting; hardware button triggers are simulated transparently for browser sandboxes; emergency dispatch is simulated locally for academic evaluation without placing real 112 distress calls. It is assumed the passenger's handset has GPS connectivity and the driver is in close physical proximity.

Scope: what the design will and will not cover

In-scope items include: a complete mobile-first 10-screen interface; interactive Leaflet route map with live telemetry; covert triggers (1.5s radial hold, secret logo tap, volume rocker simulator); Dual-PIN verification logic (Normal 1234 vs Duress 9999); Autonomous AI Route Shield with 30s countdown; 4 specialized scotopic night themes; persistent Evaluator Demo Lab dock. Out-of-scope items include commercial cellular SMS carrier billing, direct live dispatch to municipal police control rooms, and in-cabin hardware camera recording.

2. User Research

Stage: Discover. Gather evidence from real people. Interview or observe at least five users from the target group.

Research method and questions asked

Semi-structured interviews and scenario walkthroughs were conducted with 5 real users under simulated low-light conditions. Key questions included: (1) Have you ever felt uncomfortable or threatened during a night cab ride? How did you respond? (2) What prevents you from pressing the SOS button in existing ride apps? (3) What would you do if a driver asked why your phone is flashing an alert and demanded you cancel it? (4) How important is being able to trigger an alert from your pocket without looking at the screen?

Participant details

The participant group comprised real student commuters and tech professionals: Shreyas M (21, Final Year B.E. student), Sivanesan (22, Tech Intern commuting at night), Yuvan (21, Regular night cab commuter), Samarth (22, Student researcher), and Divya (24, Night-shift corporate professional).

Key findings and direct observations

All participants confirmed they avoid conventional in-app panic buttons because audible sirens or conspicuous red banners would provoke the driver. In a dark car interior, screen brightness reflects prominently on the windshield and rear-view mirror. Crucially, participants expressed extreme vulnerability to driver coercion, noting that an attacker could easily force them to cancel an active alert.

Competitor or existing solution analysis

Commercial solutions (Uber, Ola, Rapido) offer panic buttons that either dial emergency voice calls openly or display conspicuous banners. None provide coercion-resistant duress states, silent background escalation, or scotopic display ergonomics.

Insights that will drive the design

Distress activation must be 100% silent; a 1.5-second hold gesture prevents false alarms; and a dedicated Duress PIN (9999) must deceive an attacker with a peaceful interface while maintaining high-priority response.

4. User Journey and Information Architecture

Stage: Define. Map how the user moves from need to outcome and organise the content and features to support it.

End-to-end user journey with emotional highs and lows

The passenger journey spans five distinct phases: (1) Boarding Cab (Neutral / Vigilant): Verifies driver plate (KA 03 MG 4821) and enables Night Owl auto-share. (2) Route Detour (High Anxiety / Panic): Vehicle diverts 450m off designated corridor into unlit lane; AI Route Shield triggers assistance prompt. (3) Covert Activation (Controlled Stealth): Passenger holds SOS for 1.5s or triple-taps logo; silent packet transmitted; screen disguised as calculator. (4) Coercion / Hostage Deception (Deceptive Calm): Driver demands phone; passenger enters Duress PIN 9999; UI displays peaceful green *"Trip In Progress"*; internal police dispatch escalates. (5) Resolution (Relieved / Safe): Response unit intercepts vehicle; passenger safely recovered.

Task flows for the three most critical tasks

  1. Task 1 (Covert SOS Activation): Active Ride HUD → Safety Hub → Press & Hold SOS (1.5s radial ring fills 0% to 100%) → Silent SOS Dispatched (Zero Sound) → Optional Calculator Camouflage.
  2. Task 2 (Duress PIN Deception): Driver Demands Phone → Keypad Opened → Enters Duress PIN 9999 → UI Shows Peaceful Green *"Trip In Progress"* → Backend Covert Dispatch Escalated.
  3. Task 3 (Autonomous AI Watchdog): Vehicle Diverges >400m → Tactile Ping → 30s Countdown Prompt → Confirms *"I am Safe"* (Resets) or Auto-SOS Dispatched on Expiry.

Annotations explaining layout and interaction decisions

Emergency action blocks are anchored in the bottom 35% of the screen for natural thumb reach during one-handed use. Continuous circular SVG progress visualizes hold duration (0% to 100%) so users know exactly when distress registers. Numeric keypad buttons feature generous 64px hit-boxes to accommodate trembling fingers under acute distress.

Quick feedback from at least two people

Reviewer 1 (UX Peer) noted that the SOS button in early sketches looked too similar to a standard ride cancellation button. Reviewer 2 (Faculty Advisor) advised ensuring the PIN keypad explicitly explains the difference between accidental review and duress for evaluation.

Changes made after feedback

Added an explicit emergency rose border and pulsed radio icon to differentiate Silent SOS from normal ride actions. Integrated evaluator shortcut auto-fill buttons (*"Auto-fill Normal PIN 1234"* and *"Auto-fill Duress PIN 9999"*) at the base of the keypad.

9. Usability Testing and Iteration

Stage: Test. Test the prototype with at least five users on defined tasks, then improve it. Include an accessibility review against WCAG 2.2 AA.

Test plan: tasks, participants and success criteria

Five participants (Shreyas M, Sivanesan, Yuvan, Samarth, and Divya) tested the interactive prototype under dim lighting. Tasks: (1) Activate Silent SOS via 1.5s hold; (2) Cancel accidental alert with Normal PIN 1234; (3) Deactivate under driver coercion with Duress PIN 9999. Success Criteria: sub-3s trigger time, 100% completion, zero audio alerts.

Results: task success, time on task, errors and satisfaction

Average time on Task 1 was 2.4 seconds; Task 2 was 3.1 seconds; Task 3 was 2.8 seconds. Task completion was 100% (15/15 trials). The System Usability Scale (SUS) scored an outstanding 89.5 / 100.

Accessibility check and issues fixed

All body copy satisfies WCAG 2.2 AA contrast (>7:1); touch targets exceed 48px; keyboard navigation focus outlines and aria-label tags implemented.

Problems found, ranked by severity

Severity 1 (High - Fixed): CartoDB map tiles produced "API KEY REQUIRED" watermarks; replaced with free OpenStreetMap tiles with custom CSS night filters. Severity 2 (Medium - Fixed): Users were unsure how long to hold the button; added dynamic percentage text in the center of the radial ring. Severity 3 (Low - Fixed): Desktop view stretched excessively; added a persistent smartphone frame mockup.

Design changes made and evidence of improvement

Added radial hold percentage counter, free OpenStreetMap tiles, smartphone viewport frame toggle, and Evaluator Demo Lab dock.

10. Design Rationale, Reflection and Future Work

Stage: Reflect. Defend your hardest decisions and be honest about what remains unsolved.

Major design decisions and trade-offs

A single tap is faster (0.2s), but user research revealed passengers fear accidental triggers that summon police. Enforcing a 1.5s hold with progress feedback provided the optimal balance between panic speed and false alarm mitigation. In coercion engineering, conventional UI transparency is fatal. Displaying a peaceful green ride screen under Duress PIN entry intentionally deceives the attacker to protect the passenger's life.

Limitations of the final design

Hardware volume buttons can only be intercepted in web browsers when the browser tab is focused; native OS background service integration requires a hybrid mobile wrapper. Real cellular telephony requires an external SMS gateway.

What failed or was redone, and what was learned

Initial use of CartoDB tiles failed with "API KEY REQUIRED" watermarks due to new token policies. We resolved this by integrating free OpenStreetMap tiles with custom GPU-accelerated CSS filters, underscoring the critical necessity of vendor independence in safety systems.

Ethical considerations

SafeRide operates under a strict non-eavesdropping policy (zero unpermitted microphone recording); dummy PINs are handled strictly in local memory; and simulated emergency dispatch is watermarked clearly for academic review.

Future improvements

  • BLE Smart Jewelry: Integration with tactile smart rings or keyfob panic buttons.
  • On-Device Edge NLP: Client-side acoustic distress keyword detection running on WebAssembly.
  • Automotive OBD-II Integration: Bi-directional communication with vehicle control units to disable child locks during an emergency.
